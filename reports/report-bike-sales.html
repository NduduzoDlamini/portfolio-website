Business Intelligence & Data Analytics

A written breakdown of the approach, key findings, and recommendations from this project.

    Overview

    This project analyzes a dataset of 1,000 bike buyer records to understand what drives bike purchase decisions across demographic, economic, and geographic factors. Using Microsoft Excel for data cleaning, transformation, and PivotTable analysis, the project explores how income, gender, age, commute distance, region, and marital status relate to whether a customer purchased a bike. The workbook includes a working sheet with calculated fields (age brackets) and a PivotTable sheet with grouped summaries, culminating in a dashboard for decision-makers.

    Key Findings

    Finding 1 — Purchasers earn more than non-purchasers

    Average income for those who purchased a bike is $57,963**, compared to **$54,875 for those who did not — a difference of roughly $3,088 (5.6%). Income is a measurable differentiator, though not the only one.

    Finding 2 — Middle-aged buyers dominate purchases

    Of the 481 total purchases, 383 came from the "Middle age" bracket (31–54), representing nearly 80% of all buyers. Adolescents (under 31) purchased only 39 bikes, and older customers (55+) purchased 59. The middle-aged segment is clearly the core market.

    Finding 3 — Short commutes drive the most purchases

    Buyers with a 0–1 mile commute made 200 purchases — the highest of any distance band, and more than the next two bands combined. Purchase counts drop steadily as commute distance increases, with only 33 purchases among riders commuting 10+ miles. This suggests bikes are being bought for short, local trips rather than long commutes.

    Finding 4 — Male buyers have higher average income and purchase slightly more

    Males who purchased had an average income of $60,124**, versus **$55,774 for females who purchased. Overall purchase counts (481 of 1,000) skew slightly toward males in the dataset, and male non-purchasers still had a higher average income ($56,208) than female non-purchasers ($53,440).

    Methodology

    • Data source: A single Excel workbook with four sheets — bike_buyers (raw data), Working Sheet (with an added Age Brackets calculated column), Pivot table (summarized groupings), and Dashboard (visual layout).
    • Data cleaning: Converted abbreviated category labels in the raw sheet (e.g., "M"/"S"/"F" for Marital Status and Gender, "10+ Miles" for commute distance) into full-text labels (Married/Single/Female, More than 10 Miles). Created the Age Brackets column using a nested IF formula: =IF(L2>54,"Old", IF(L2>=31,"Middle age", IF(L2<31,"Adolescent", "invalid"))).
    • Summarization: Built PivotTables to aggregate Average of Income by Gender × Purchased Bike, Count of Purchased Bike by Commute Distance, and Count of Purchased Bike by Age Brackets.
    • Visualization: Assembled a dashboard (sheet 4) to present the pivot outputs for stakeholder review.
    • Validation: Cross-checked that the sum of the Count of Purchased Bike pivot equals 1,000 (the full dataset), and that "Yes" + "No" totals reconcile across every grouping.

    Recommendations

    • Target middle-aged buyers as the primary marketing segment — Since 80% of purchases come from the 31–54 age bracket, align ad spend, messaging, and product positioning with this demographic. Younger and older segments are smaller opportunities that may require different product lines (e.g., commuter vs. leisure models).
    • Emphasize short-commute convenience in messaging — With 200 of 481 purchases coming from 0–1 mile commuters, marketing should stress "quick trips," "last-mile convenience," and "around-the-neighborhood" use cases rather than long-distance touring.
    • Launch targeted campaigns for female buyers — Females who purchased averaged $4,350 less income than males who purchased, and female non-purchasers averaged lower income still. Consider pricing tiers or financing options to widen access for this under-penetrated segment.
